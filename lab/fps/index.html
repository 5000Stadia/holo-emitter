What it says so far

  • How we build beats which engine. In every engine, one batch is about 10× faster than many meshes.
  • Godot's lead per object is gone with render bundles. Recording the draws once (WebGPU only): 1,000 separate meshes went from 148 to 1,337 fps, and 10,000 from 12 to 247. The busy street (5,000 objects, 50 meshes) went from 23 to 330; Godot draws it at 35.
  • The busy street, batched per mesh type (about 51 draws): three.js 1,342 fps, Godot 658. With shadows: three.js 559, Godot 525.
  • Shadows: the sun's shadow map drawn once took three.js from 86 to 136 fps (Godot 170); only big things casting, 86 to 106. Small things can do without casting.
  • A shared material took three.js from 148 to 401 fps (Godot 217 to 313). Frozen matrices made no difference.
  • Pixels: capping pixel density at 1× made a pixel-heavy scene 3.2× faster (227 to 721 fps). Drawing at half resolution and upscaling with FSR1 was slower (174) on this GPU.
  • Candles: 64 point lights cost about 25% with three.js's default lighting; clustered lighting (WebGPU only) gets nearly all of it back (147 against 117 fps). Batched dynamic lighting is no faster per frame, but lighting or snuffing a candle recompiles nothing.
  • Far away, ordinary depth loses detail at 4 km; logarithmic depth holds it at no measurable cost in this draw-bound scene. Since measured (2026-10-08, below): where fill counts, log depth costs 37–55% of the GPU frame, and reversed depth holds the same 4 km view, pixel for pixel, at no cost.
  • three.js is faster on raw geometry (23M triangles at 66 fps against 44) and on physics (Rapier 3.1 ms a step against 4.7 for 500 boxes).
  • Godot's web export uses its Compatibility renderer: no ambient occlusion, no visible point lights, no clearcoat. Its web engine is a 39.5 MB download; three.js's WebGPU build is 0.8 MB.

Phones, 2026-10-08: the manor's phone measures, one at a time

The night's optimisations on the real manor, each brought back to the lab's own scene and changed one at a time from a reference, m-ref, the manor's desktop set. That is 1,000 instanced spheres in the kit's textures (1024² colour, normal and roughness), a board floor at 360 px/m (2880²) and 8 window lights (RectAreaLight). It also has 4× MSAA, ACES tone mapping, a half-float intermediate target, ordinary depth, and the far town out to 5 km.

Each case was run 5 times, round-robin, on this machine's GPU in headless Chrome. The numbers swing here, so each cell is the median, with the spread (lowest to highest of the 5) after it.

The first number is GPU ms a frame (WebGPU timestamps), the number fill and shading move. Under it come fps (frame gaps with vsync off, CPU and GPU together, the noisier one) and the change against m-ref. Green is a saving that holds beyond the spread; red is a cost that does.

Desktop is 1280×720 at 1×. Phone is 390×844 at DPR 3, so 1170×2532 pixels are drawn, 3.2× the desktop's. GPU ms are timed apart from the fps run: 40 frames along the same orbit, each drawn alone and its timestamps awaited. WebGL 2's timer query answered on the desktop runs but seldom at the phone's size, so there WebGL 2 is judged by its frame time. Other sessions' headless browsers shared this GPU tonight, which is why the fps spreads are wide; the serial GPU ms held within about 2%. Godot's web build (Compatibility renderer) has no depth, output-target or pipeline switches, and those cells are empty.
