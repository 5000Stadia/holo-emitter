Now · R49, a place from a book

Alice's hall of doors new
Chapter I of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, from the book's text to a hall you play. One AI step read the chapter, quoting it for every fact; after that nothing is AI. The hall is laid out, built, furnished and checked by the same system as the manor. You are the size the story makes you: ten inches after the bottle, more than nine feet after the cake, stooping under the roof. Everything said is Carroll's own sentence.
Try: take the golden key from the glass table · find the low curtain at the far end, draw it aside, and unlock the little door · go back to the table · leave the key there and drink · then find your way into the garden. E/click uses a thing, or puts down what you hold on the table.

R47, a house built by its purpose

The manor, room by room
A 1660 gentry seat with 35 rooms on three floors, laid out from what such a household needed, carved from one solid so no wall can crack, every stair railed wherever it drops. Each room is built by its type: flags or boards, panelling, limewash or tapestry, beams or plaster, its hearth, and the furniture that names it, all from period inventories. Every door, curtain, lid and leaf works. Only the rooms you can see through open doorways are drawn.
Try: walk from the hall through the screens passage to the kitchen (the spit turns when you wind the jack) · up the great stair to the great chamber, hung with tapestry · draw back the bed curtains in the best bedchamber · raise the gateleg's leaf in the little parlour · climb to the long gallery. E/click uses a thing.
The banqueting house
A garden banqueting house of c.1660, where the party withdrew for the sweet course: one exact octagonal room, windows in its diagonal sides, a chimneypiece, the draw-table through its middle and chairs ranged round its walls. Rooms of any shape are now carved from one solid, so no wall can crack, and furnished by rules each piece declares.
Try: walk in from the lobby · walk at a diagonal wall or the fire (you stop) · open the draw-table's leaves.
The manor's furniture new
The 22 pieces that name the rooms, each a data recipe from the inventories: the long table and forms, the court cupboard, the standing bed, the hogshead with its tap, the kitchen's jack and spit, the bread oven, and the rest.
Try: click whatever looks as if it works · add &k=table/gateleg (or any other piece's name) to see one piece alone.

R48, the production system

The strongroom everything works
The 1660 muniment room built from its period brief. Every piece of furniture is made from a data recipe, and everything that looks as if it works, works.
Try: pull a labelled drawer · open the table drawer and take the key · the key opens the chest's padlocks · close all four shutters, and the room goes dark until you light the candle · the gallery door is locked and its key isn't here. E/click uses a thing, G takes it, B shows the brief.
The test room new
One piece three ways, as a house will ask of it: back to a wall, in a corner and in a narrow passage, each stood off the wall by what swings behind it. Its lids and doors open and close by themselves. On a phone the camera visits each in turn. Every piece is now checked when it is made: every part closed, faces out, nothing floating, buried, through another or into the wall, at rest and opened.
Try: change k= to chest/boarded, tub/powdering, wheel/spinning, cabinet/japanned or any piece's name · ask whether each reads as a real 1660 thing.
Light three candles new
Locks and rules: a locked door and its key, and a door that opens when 3 of the 4 candles are lit.
Try: take the key (G or shift-click), then click the left door · light the candles one by one.
Play never waits new
Alice's DRINK ME bottle, as a kind whose label hasn't been written yet. It stands as holodeck grid at its true size, then turns real when the label arrives three seconds later.
Try: watch the grid become the bottle · G takes it either way.
Every kind new
Each small object type in the catalogue, side by side on a bench, for approving in a batch.
Try: lift the tankard's and the box's lids · turn the globe · light a candle.
Three keepers' shelves
A poor widow, a blacksmith and a great gentleman. The same shelf rules, bent by who owns the shelf.
Try: click the things on them: they're objects in their own right.
Pepys's bookpress
A glazed bookcase after Samuel Pepys's of 1666, with carved acanthus and books ordered by size.
Try: open its four glazed doors.
A library wall
Four bays and about 600 books from the same book recipe, built in a fraction of a second.
Try: add &means=poor or &purpose=chapel to the address and watch the shelves change.

Checks you can run

The fps lab ledger new
One test scene in three.js (WebGPU and WebGL 2) and in Godot 4.7's web export, changing one thing at a time: objects, triangles, instancing, lights, shadows, materials, post effects, physics. Frames per second and milliseconds for each, with which comparisons are fair.
Read: how we build matters more than which engine; Godot's web renderer skips several effects; Godot's web engine is a 40 MB download.
Forest scale test new
87,000 trees on a kilometre of hillside, in 11 draw calls, on WebGPU (WebGL 2 where there isn't one). It tests speed, not looks: the trees are crude on purpose.
Try: walk with WASD (shift runs) · the top-left panel shows fps on your device · add ?webgl=1 to compare.
The bench phone
Rebuilds the strongroom, the shelves, the bookpress, the library and every kind, and checks each layout matches the reference to 0.1 mm. Then it times the builds and frame rates on this device.
Try: open it on the phone and on the laptop · a minute each · "copy the results" if anything differs.
