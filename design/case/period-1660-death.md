# A sudden death in an English gentry house, c.1660 (M7 period notes)

Tags: **[S]** sourced from a page opened this session; **[snip]** seen only in a search-result summary; **[C]** chosen for the game, not sourced; **[gap]** no source found. The 1660s evidence I reached is thin; much procedure is documented from the 16th or 18th century and carried across. Vet **[C]** items before they shape a clue.

## 1. The death: coroner, jury, constable, justice

- **Who acts.** Whoever finds a body of a sudden or unnatural death must raise the hue and cry and tell the coroner [S, [Wikipedia: Coroner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coroner)]. The coroner issues a warrant; the **parish constable** summons the jury [S, [London Lives: Coroners' Inquests](https://old.londonlives.org/static/IC.jsp), 18th c.]. The coroner is an elected county officer; by Tudor times his almost only function was inquests [snip, search summary].
- **When and where.** Within about 48 hours; "usually at a local alehouse, parish workhouse, or the building where the death occurred" [S, London Lives, 18th c.]. So an inquest in the house, or in the nearest inn, is attested; **choose** the hall or great parlour, with the body laid out [C].
- **Jury.** 12 to 24 substantial householders of the parish [S, London Lives]; a sampled 148 sixteenth-century juries leaned to "middling" men but did not exclude rich or poor [snip, [Gunn & Gromelski, abstract](https://www.history.ox.ac.uk/node/2938746) (page 404 on fetch; abstract via search)]. Jurors would know the household and its gossip [S, [How to History](https://howtohistory.substack.com/p/coroners-inquests)].
- **View of the body.** The formula "upon view of the body" was required (and sometimes falsely claimed) [snip, [Legal History Miscellany](https://legalhistorymiscellany.com/2017/07/09/deaths-in-custody/): lead]. Jurors examined corpse and scene, questioned family and friends [S, How to History]. **Gameplay use [C]:** the player is the coroner's clerk or a gentleman invited to attend; the jury's view is a scene before the first question.
- **Written evidence.** Taken in writing "only in cases of homicide"; on a homicide verdict the coroner takes recognisances for a prosecutor and witnesses [snip from search; the opened [Legal History Miscellany article](https://legalhistorymiscellany.com/2018/12/26/prosecuting-homicide-on-the-coroners-inquisition/) confirms recognisances and the inquisition as a bill like a grand jury's, but is about the 18th–19th c.]. Murder goes on to the assizes: the inquisition is produced at once, indictment may follow months later [S, same article].
- **Justice of the Peace.** Dalton's *The Country Justice* (1618; a 1666 edition is catalogued) was the JP's manual and covers murder and felony [S, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Country_Justice)]. Its procedure for examinations and committal I could not read [gap]. **Chosen [C]:** the JP is a neighbouring gentleman who takes statements, can bind witnesses and commit a suspect to gaol until the assizes; the player's role is to give him grounds.
- **Verdicts.** Felo de se; non compos mentis (property not forfeit); accident/misadventure; homicide (murder, manslaughter, chance-medley) [S, [Wikipedia: Felo de se](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felo_de_se); London Lives; a search summary also attests "deodand" objects named in misadventure]. A felo de se verdict forfeits goods and brings a shameful burial at a crossroads [S, Wikipedia]; since 1510 coroners were paid a mark per homicide verdict including felo de se, deducted from forfeited goods, a documented incentive to find suicide [snip]. Over the 17th–18th c. juries moved towards non compos mentis [S: Wikipedia, citing MacDonald & Murphy: by the 1710s over 90% of suicides were judged insane]. "Died by the visitation of God" (natural death) is standard in registers but I found no source for it in this search [gap, **[C]** use it].
- **Plot hooks that follow from the sources [C]:** forfeiture means a heir benefits from "natural" or "accident" and loses from felo de se; a jury of the house's own tenants is a pressure point; a burial before the inquest was an offence (18th c., snippet only).

## 2. Land, deeds, mortgages

- **Tenures Abolition Act 1660 (12 Cha. II c. 24)** ended the Court of Wards and knight-service tenures, converting land to socage, and removed wardship of heirs [snip, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenures_Abolition_Act_1660): lead].
- **Strict settlement** was the usual gentry form: the owner is only tenant for life, trustees hold the estate, each generation gets a new entail naming the eldest son; it could not be sold without agreement of heirs and trustees [S, [Univ. of Nottingham deeds guide](https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/manuscriptsandspecialcollections/researchguidance/deedsindepth/settlements/description.aspx)]. Settlements are tied to marriage (ante-nuptial) and provide portions for younger children [S]. Re-breaking each generation is a post-1736 habit; **choose [C]** a settlement made at the heir's marriage.
- **Common recovery**: a collusive suit that converts entailed land to fee simple so it can be sold or mortgaged: owner conveys to a "tenant to the praecipe"; a friendly claimant sues; the owner vouches a "common vouchee" who defaults; judgment for the buyer [S, [Wikipedia: Common recovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_recovery); a 1633 East Sussex example is held at Birmingham]. Common Pleas processed them [snip, inference]. **Fine** in the conveyancing sense is another collusive suit with a record; the Bedfordshire catalogue shows a 1665–66 fine on manors before Orlando Bridgman as Chief Justice of Common Pleas [snip, [Bedfordshire Archives](https://bedsarchivescat.bedford.gov.uk/Details/archive/110034024): lead]. **Clue idea [C, vet]:** a sale or settlement that lacks the recovery/fine its deed claims is void; a forged deed would have to explain the missing record. I found no source on forgery detection [gap].
- **Mortgage.** A conveyance of the land to the lender with a redemption day; miss the day and the land was lost at law though the debt stood. Chancery protected the borrower's **equity of redemption** and made a mortgagee in possession account for rents (*Holman v Vaux* 1616, *Pell v Blewet* 1630) [S, [Wikipedia: Mortgages in English law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortgages_in_English_law)]. The doctrine's origin date is disputed [snip]. **Clue idea [C]:** a mortgage past its day, with a lender who quietly holds the deed: motive and leverage.

## 3. Steward and household

- **Steward.** Paid, but counted as part of the family [snip, Cambridge Core "Stewards, Lords and People", lead]. Estate stewards held manorial courts (court baron every 3–4 weeks), raised fines on copyholds, kept the yearly "charge and discharge" accounts, Michaelmas to Michaelmas; a bailiff collected [snip, Notts/Beds guides]. Big estates had chief and understewards; small ones a part-time country attorney [snip]. Legal limits of his power in the 1660s: [gap]. **[C]** He has the keys to the muniment room and the rentals but cannot seal a conveyance without the owner; that gap is where a forgery lives.
- **Household ranks [gap on gentry specifics; Cliffe, *The World of the Country House in Seventeenth-Century England* (Yale 1999) is the book to consult, not opened].** **Chosen order [C]:** steward; gentleman usher or secretary; chaplain; (above-stairs) gentlewoman/waiting-woman; butler/cellarer; cook; groom/coachman; footmen/under-servants; housemaids; kitchen/dairy maids. Wages: a new cook-maid at £4 a year was the most Pepys had paid, 1663 [S, Pepys, [vol. 21, Gutenberg #4140](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4140/pg4140.txt)]; a gentry house would pay a steward far more [C].

## 4. Voices

- **Address [C, consistent with usage].** Knights and baronets: *Sir* (surname forms "Sir Henry"); other men: *Master/Mr Surname*, formally *your Worship* to a JP or a landowning justice; women: *Madam* (to a lady), *Mistress* (surname) for gentlewomen, married or not; servants speak of "my master", "my mistress", "my lady". [Pepys 1663: Lady Batten said she "would teach his mistress better manners" (S); Pepys on a new maid: "knows not how to give the respect to her mistress" (S).]
- **Thou/you.** *Thou* had been used for familiarity, contempt, or to inferiors; by about 1650 most southern English speakers had dropped it even in familiar speech, and it persisted in regions and among Quakers ("plain speaking") [S, [Wikipedia: Thou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thou); Lass quoted via search: intimate if reciprocal, contemptuous if not]. **Choose [C]:** everyone says *you* to the player; a northern old servant or a Quaker tenant says *thee/thou*; the master may *thou* a child or a dog, and never a gentleman.
- **Idiom [S for the samples, C for the rule].** Pepys reports a sick father crying "Terrible, terrible pain, God help me, God help me"; "Lord!" is Pepys's constant exclamation; Holmes calling Minnes "a knave, rogue, coward" [S, Pepys vol. 21]. Add by choice: *I pray you*, *by my troth*, *God keep you*, *an't please you*, *'tis*, *'twas*, *methinks*, *nay*, *I warrant*; mild oaths ("by God's grace", "Lord have mercy") for the pious, "'sblood" and "God's wounds" only for rough men [C, verify]. Avoid *okay*, *guys*, and *sorry* as apology (use *I crave your pardon*) [C].
- **Servant to gentleman.** Third person is the safest courtesy: "If it please your Worship...", "my master was...". Short answers, deferential repetition of the title, deflection by "I know not, sir" rather than "I don't know" [C].
- **Deposition voice.** Legal depositions run "this deponent saith..." [C, from general knowledge]. The Old Bailey *Proceedings* begin in 1674 (April sessions) with regular publication from 1678, but until the 1720s they are brief with little verbatim testimony [snip, [London Lives: OBP](https://old.londonlives.org/static/OBP.jsp)]. For persona prompts, supply 6–8 such phrases per character rather than ask the model to "speak 1660".
