# Natural plant distributions, Midlands limestone hillside, c.1660: research for holo-emitter

Tags: **[S]** read in a page I fetched. **[U]** from a search summary only. **[K]** my general botanical knowledge, not checked against a fetched page. **[INF]** my inference. Fetched pages treated as data.

## 0. Method and limits

- **Period text:** Culpeper, *Complete Herbal* (first ed. 1653), Project Gutenberg #49513, the "Place" and "Time" lines of about 235 entries, read locally **[S]**. The Gutenberg text is a later edition, so the Place/Time lines are the best 17th-c. evidence but not guaranteed 1653-original. For very common plants ("well known") he gives no place. Gerard 1597/1633 and Parkinson 1629/1640 were not readable in full (secondary only).
- **Numbers:** Ellenberg-type indicator values (L light 1-9, F moisture 1-12, R reaction/pH 1-9, N nutrients 1-9) come from the harmonised European table of Tichy et al. 2023 (Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.7427088, CC-BY 4.0; downloaded and read) **[S]**. These average several national databases, not Britain alone. "x" = indifferent; "~" = my estimate **[INF]**. PLANTATT (Hill, Preston, Roy 2004, the British set) exists as a scanned PDF only, so was unreadable.
- Grazing, disturbance and edge-affinity numbers, heights, bloom months and colours are **[K]/[INF]**, cross-checked against Culpeper "Time" where he gives one.
- Region: White Peak (limestone plateau, deep dales, flower-rich limestone grassland and ash woodland, drystone walls; Natural England NCA 52 via search **[U]**), with acid patches on shale/gritstone **[INF]**.

## 1. Habitats and what grew there

### Hedge bottoms and hedge banks
- Hedges: hawthorn, blackthorn and (older, mixed) hazel, field maple, dogwood; in 1660 expect hawthorn-dominated new quickset near the house and older mixed hedges on the closes (Wikipedia, Hedge **[S]**; field maple, hazel and dogwood mark an older hedge, North Yorks hedgerow HAP **[U]**). Hooper's rule (species per 30 yd x 110 = years) fits northern hedges poorly (Wikipedia **[S]**), so do not use it to pick species counts.
- Climbers and scramblers: dog rose, bramble, honeysuckle, ivy. Culpeper: elder planted "in hedges ... to strengthen the fences ... and hold the banks by ditches" **[S]**; bryony "on banks, or under hedges" **[S]**.
- Banks and bottoms (shade, moderate nutrients): primrose, wood anemone, ramsons, bluebell, greater stitchwort, lords-and-ladies, herb Robert, hedge parsley, cow parsley, garlic mustard; ancient hedgerows keep wood anemone, ramsons, primrose (North Yorks HAP **[U]**; Flora of Northern Ireland **[U]**; Wildlife Trusts: cow parsley and hedge mustard thick at hedge foot **[U]**).
- Culpeper: cuckoo-pint "under every hedge-side" **[S]**; ground ivy "under hedges ... shadowed lanes" **[S]**; herb Robert "by the way-sides, upon ditch banks" **[S]**; sauce-alone (garlic mustard) "under walls, and by hedge-sides" **[S]**; wild campion "by fields and hedge-sides, and ditches" **[S]**; cleavers "by the hedge and ditch-sides" **[S]**.

### Dry-stone walls: foot and top
- Foot: herb Robert, nettle, cow parsley, ivy, ferns. Face and top: biting stonecrop is among the strongest dry-wall species (45%+ of 40 Mendip walls, Bath-area study via search **[U]**); wild thyme and harebell on thin soil at the top **[K]**.
- Mortar changes it: maidenhair spleenwort and wall-rue prefer lime mortar and avoid bare dry walls (same study **[U]**). Dry-stone field walls are therefore mostly stonecrop, thyme, mosses and lichens; ferns and wallflower belong on mortared or house walls.
- Culpeper: wallflower "on church walls, and old walls of many houses" **[S]**; stonecrop "upon the stone walls and mud walls, upon the tiles" **[S]**; pellitory-of-the-wall "by the sides of walls" **[S]**; wall pennywort "especially in all the west parts" **[S]**, so rare on the Peak limestone **[INF]**.

### Field margins, headlands, arable
- Cornfield weeds are archaeophytes (pre-1500 arrivals, BSBI **[S]**): poppy, cornflower (Culpeper "blue-bottle ... in cornfields ... May to end of harvest" **[S]**), corncockle, corn marigold. Culpeper's poppy is "many times too much so" in corn and on "ditch banks, and by hedge sides" **[S]**. Gerard 1633 notes cornflower blunting sickles (via search **[U]**). Only where a ridge is under crop; thin-soiled sheep-walk has none.
- Margin perennials: knapweed (Culpeper: "fields and meadows, and about their borders and hedges" **[S]**), field scabious, hogweed, burdock, creeping thistle.

### Grazed pasture and limestone turf
- Limestone grassland: rock-rose, cowslip, salad burnet, bird's-foot trefoil, harebell, quaking grass, small scabious, wild thyme, lady's bedstraw, dropwort, bloody cranesbill (NE TIN guide via search **[U]**; White Peak flora, andydownunder **[S]**; Deep Dale, Plantlife **[U]**). Rock-rose flowers from June **[S]**; cowslip is among the first to flower **[S]**.
- Grazing: heavy sheep grazing or trampling makes rosettes; sheep eat flower heads; over-grazing leaves bare ground that favours thistles and ragwort (NE upland limestone grassland guide via search **[U]**). So close-grazed 1660 sward = short turf, rosettes (daisy, plantain, dandelion, cowslip leaves, white clover), thyme mats, scattered thistles and ragwort, few tall flowers except where fenced or low-stocked.
- Culpeper: dandelion "in all meadows and pasture-grounds"; yarrow "frequent in all pastures"; dove's-foot cranesbill "in pasture grounds, and by the path-sides"; wild thyme "commons, and other barren places" **[S]**.

### Hay meadow, ridge and furrow
- Meadow plants: knapweed, ox-eye daisy, meadow buttercup, yellow rattle, meadowsweet in wet, meadow cranesbill. Yellow rattle sets seed by early July and "farmers used to use the sound of the rattling seeds as their cue to cut the hay" (Plantlife; NE TIN060 via search **[U]**). Upland recipe: spring and autumn grazing, mid-July cut (review via search **[U]**). Cut in July, so a July/August scene shows meadow stubble and aftermath, not flowers **[INF]**.
- Ridge and furrow: dry ridges hold cowslip, knapweed, quaking grass; damp furrows hold cuckooflower, ragged-robin, adder's-tongue; occasionally reversed (Great Oakley Meadow; Woodsides; Pilch Field; Asham Meads, via search **[U]**). Rushes and meadowsweet in the wettest furrows **[INF]**.

### Trackways, verges, gateways
- Trampled, nutrient-rich: greater plantain, knotgrass ("by the highway sides, and by foot-paths in fields" **[S]**), silverweed, docks, nettle, white clover, dandelion, chickweed, mugwort. Tall verge: mullein ("by way-sides and lanes" **[S]**), burdock ("by ditches ... and by the highways" **[S]**), hogweed, hedge-mustard, weld ("by the way sides ... bye lanes" **[S]**). Docks and nettles mark gateways and middens **[K]**.

### Damp hollows, springs, ditches
- Meadowsweet ("moist meadows" **[S]**), cuckooflower ("moist places, and near to brooksides" **[S]**), yellow flag ("watery ditches, ponds" **[S]**), marsh marigold, soft rush, water mint, angelica, willow. Hops in "low moist grounds" **[S]**. Rare Peak specials (globeflower, east of Buxton) skip.

### Woodland edge, shade under hedgerow trees
- Ash is the characteristic Peak limestone tree. Under it and at wood edges: dog's mercury, ramsons, bluebell, wood anemone, primrose, wood sorrel (Culpeper "woods ... moist and shadowed" **[S]**), periwinkle ("woods and orchards, by the hedge-sides ... March and April" **[S]**), bugle, foxglove on acid soils (Culpeper "dry sandy ground ... under hedge-sides" **[S]**).

### Scrub, rough corners
- Hawthorn, blackthorn, dog rose and bramble thickets; juniper on limestone commons (Culpeper lists commons **[S]**; Peak dales **[K]**); gorse on acid patches (Culpeper "dry barren heaths ... gravelly or sandy" **[S]**); broom "commonly" **[S]**; bracken on acid slopes; elder near habitation and middens (nitrogen N8.0 **[S]**, Culpeper **[S]**). Willow in wet corners.

### House, forecourt, yard
- In gravel and joints: knotgrass, pineapple weed is post-1800 (omit), plantain, groundsel, shepherd's purse, stonecrop, thyme. Round buildings: nettle, dock, elder, henbane and hemlock on rubbish (Culpeper has both **[S]**), pellitory-of-the-wall, mallow, feverfew **[K]**. Houseleek on stone slate roofs (Culpeper: "tiles of houses" **[S]**).

### Orchard floor
- Mown or grazed grass with daisy, dandelion, cowslip, primrose, white clover, plantain, periwinkle ("in woods and orchards" **[S]**), fruit trees (apple, pear, cherry, quince, plum, damson); Parkinson's 1629 title promises "an orchard of all sorte of fruitbearing Trees and shrubbes" **[S]**.

## 2. What would look wrong
- Post-1500 neophytes, not yet here (dates): Rhododendron ponticum, 1763 (Wikipedia **[S]**); Himalayan balsam 1839 **[S]**; Japanese knotweed to Kew 1850, earliest wild record 1872 **[S]**; giant hogweed 1817 **[S]**; buddleia introduced 1896, wild by 1922 (BSBI via search **[U]**); Oxford ragwort introduced c.1700 **[S]**; ivy-leaved toadflax garden 1617, first outside cultivation 1640, so absent from a Derbyshire farm wall **[S]**; sycamore on records by 1500, wild 1632 in Kent, usually a neophyte, so rare or absent **[S]**.
- Rosebay willowherb: "up until the mid-eighteenth century ... a garden plant which occasionally escaped" (Flora Britannica, via a blog **[U]**); it spread with railways and wartime clearings. Omit as a mass plant.
- Not escaped: lupins, montbretia, pampas grass, Michaelmas daisy, Canadian goldenrod, snowberry, cherry laurel, Spanish bluebell, red valerian, hydrangea, cotoneaster **[K]**. Modern wildflower-mix species like cornflower and corncockle thrive only in crops, never in "meadow patches" **[INF]**.
- Sown swards: perennial ryegrass leys and white-clover mixes are later improvements; 1660 pasture is fescue, bent, crested dog's-tail, quaking grass **[K]**.

## 3. Garden plants (designed beds only, brief)
Parkinson 1629: "Carnations and Gilloflowers bee the chiefest flowers of account in all our English Gardens" **[S]**; tulips (120 varieties), lilies, crown imperial, cyclamen, daffodils, crocus **[S]**. Knot edging/infill: germander, marjoram, thyme, southernwood, hyssop, costmary, rosemary, santolina, calendula, viola; box came in the early 17th c. and Parkinson 1629 was the first English recommendation (Wikipedia, Knot garden **[S]**). Core kit: box, lavender, rosemary, clove pinks and gillyflowers, roses (red, white, damask), thyme, hyssop, marjoram, violets, cowslips, wallflowers, tulips **[S/K]**.

## 4. Schema: a plant pool as data

```
plant: { id, name, status:"native|archaeophyte|garden|post1660",
  pref:{ light:[lo,hi] (1-9), moist:[lo,hi] (1-12), ph:[lo,hi] (1-9), fert:[lo,hi] (1-9) },
  tol:{ graze:0-1, disturb:0-1 },        // graze 1 = thrives under close sheep; disturb = trampling/plough/cut
  edge:0-1,                              // 1 = only within ~1.5 m of hedge, wall or tree
  habitats:[tags],                       // HB hedge bank, WL wall foot, WT wall top, FM margin, PS pasture, HM meadow,
                                         // RF ridge/furrow, TV track/gate, DM damp, WE wood edge, SC scrub, HS house, OR orchard
  form:"single|clump|drift|mat|runner|thicket|line",  // placement pattern
  patch:{ count:[min,max], radius_m }, h_m:[min,max],
  bloom:{ months:[start,end], colour:"#hex" }, block:0|1|2 }   // 0 none, 1 slows, 2 impassable
```
Cell context per sample point: `light` (shade of nearest tree/hedge/wall), `moist` (slope, hollow, spring map), `ph` (limestone 7.5 / acid patch 4), `fert` (distance to gate, midden, house), `trample`, `grazing`, `edgeDist`. Suitability = product over axes of `exp(-((v-mid)/halfwidth)^2)` using the plant's range, times `tol` fits, times `edge * exp(-edgeDist/1.5)` blend; draw plants by weighted choice per cell, then place by `form` (Poisson-disc seeds for clump/drift, random walk for runner, line along the hedge for `line`). Season = `bloom` months; scale flower coverage by a bell over the months. Zones are sampled once at generation (cheap arithmetic).

## 6. Defaults for the generator
- Most of the hillside is close-grazed turf with rosettes, thyme mats and scattered thistle and ragwort; flower drifts belong to fenced closes, hedge banks, wall bases and field headlands. Hedge-foot and wall-foot bands (0-1.5 m) carry most of the wild colour: stitchwort and primrose (Mar-Jun), campion and herb Robert (May-Sep).
- Blocking shrubs (hawthorn, blackthorn, bramble, gorse, juniper) only in hedge-lines, corners and scrub patches, never as isolated single bushes mid-pasture except old hawthorns.
- Seasonal story: Feb-Mar hazel catkins; Mar-Apr blackthorn, primrose, coltsfoot; Apr-May cowslip, bluebell, cow parsley, hawthorn blossom; Jun-Jul rock-rose, campion, foxglove, dog rose, poppy in corn, meadowsweet; Jul-Sep thyme, harebell, knapweed, thistle, bramble fruit; Oct sloes and haws, ivy flowers.
