## 1. THE PROGRAM

### 1.1 Which plan fits

- **Old plan.** Hall with a screens passage, service rooms at the low end, and parlour and great chamber at the high end, in an H, E or courtyard layout. Hardwick (1590s) and Haddon (long gallery added late 1500s) are Derbyshire examples. In the 1670s Ham was still an H-plan; Ham's 1672-74 alterations infilled the wings to make it double-pile. (Wikipedia, Ham House; Haddon Hall)
- **New plan.** Double-pile, compact, with a central entrance hall holding the stair, service in a semi-basement, a corridor, and back stairs. Pratt's Coleshill (c.1650-62; the date varies by source **[U]**) and Clarendon House (1664-67) are the models, and Belton (1685-87) copied Clarendon for a Lincolnshire gentry family. Pratt's own reason for the double-pile (Gunther p.24, quoted in Wikipedia, Roger Pratt): "much room in a little compass ... and there may be a great spare of walling". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Pratt_(architect)
- **Midlands gentry reality.** Eyam Hall, Derbyshire gritstone, built 1671-76 by Thomas Wright: Jacobean in look, mullioned windows, a flagged entrance hall with a fireplace, an oak stair up to a tapestry-hung room, and a nursery. It is not Pratt-style. https://www.greatbritishlife.co.uk/magazines/derbyshire/24205261.history-17th-century-eyam-hall-derbyshire/ ; Eyam Hall's own history via https://www.britainexpress.com/attractions.htm?attraction=2279 **[U for dates]**.
  - Sudbury (George Vernon, c.1660-91) is the Derbyshire hybrid: Jacobean-looking brick E-plan, but with a Restoration great stair (Pierce, c.1676), saloon, drawing room and long gallery. State rooms are on the west side, service on the east. (Wikipedia, Sudbury Hall; britainexpress Sudbury)
- **[INF] Recommendation.** For a 1660 Midlands seat with many manors, use the hybrid: a hall retained as entrance and common room, parlours beside it, a state apartment upstairs reached by a stair, and the service end. Offer a double-pile semi-basement variant for the "modern" owner. A pure Coleshill is a southern or courtly model, but it did reach the gentry (Coleshill for Pratt's cousin; Belton, which Wikipedia calls a gentry house).

### 1.3 Counts and scale (greater gentry, Lancashire)

From ASHMORE p.61-62:
- Bedchambers: 37 (Dunkenhalgh 1679), 28 (Lytham 1634), 24 (Rufford 1620). The sample is esquire and above, so a mid-gentry Derbyshire house should be a fraction of this **[INF]**.
- Resident staff: 20-30 (Rufford 1620), 30+ (Dunkenhalgh).
- Rooms named for people, e.g. "Mr Stanley's Chamber" (steward or butler) and "Cooks Chamber".
- Rooms named by colour: Purple, Green, Red and White Chambers (Bradshaw Hall 1694).
- Chambers over service rooms: over the kitchen, dairy, stable, kiln.
- Fireplaces at Dunkenhalgh 1679: 20 of the bedchambers, both dining rooms, drawing room, two parlours, kitchen, washhouse, meal house, cooler house (ASHMORE p.86). Many servants' rooms had none.
- Coleshill block: 124 x 62 ft, 9 bays, basement + 2 main floors + attic (RBH; Wikipedia says about 120 x 60 ft).
- Storeys: Coleshill's two main floors are of equal height, with no piano nobile (Wikipedia, Coleshill). Hardwick is the opposite: each storey higher than the one below, great chamber ceiling about 25 ft (Wikipedia; sophieploeg.com/blog/hardwick-hall). Horseheath basement 9 ft (Historic England research report 7654, via search summary **[U]**).
- **[GAP]** No sourced room dimensions for a hall or parlour. The builder must choose: Coleshill 62 ft deep for two rooms plus hall implies about 20-28 ft rooms **[INF]**.

### 1.4 Adjacency and sequence rules (what the sources support)

1. **Hall to service.** Screens passage with 2-3 doors on the side opposite the hall, opening to buttery, kitchen and pantry (Wikipedia, Buttery). Haddon: kitchen with pantry and buttery on either side (haddonhall.co.uk; britishheritage.com). Buttery has a stair to the beer cellar.
2. **Hall is no longer the dining room.** At Dunkenhalgh 1679 the hall "was clearly not used for eating, but had become an entrance room" (ASHMORE p.75). Servants still dined in it at Worden 1643, where there was a "servants table" (ASHMORE p.73), until the basement servants' hall (Coleshill).
3. **Hall to parlours.** Great parlour and little or living parlour sit off the hall. Great parlour held the best furniture (Oakwell, Wikipedia; ASHMORE p.79-80). Where the hall holds the stair (Coleshill), the great parlour is behind it and the dining chamber is above the parlour.
4. **Great stair to great chamber.** Great chamber (dining chamber) is on the first floor, reached by the stair. Pictures hang at the stair-head by the Great Dining Room door (Dunkenhalgh 1702, ASHMORE p.82). Hardwick: wide stone stair to the state rooms, great chamber with frieze.
5. **State apartment sequence.** Great chamber, withdrawing chamber, bedchamber, closet. Ham 1670s: suites either side of the Marble Dining Room, Withdrawing Room to Dining Room, Duchess's Bedchamber and Duke's Closet, plus a hidden door from the Private Closet to the White Closet and Volury (NT, via search summary **[U]**). Ham: Queen's Apartment as a three-room suite (Wikipedia, Ham House).
6. **Closets** sit off bedchambers; close stools were often in the closet or inner chamber (Dunkenhalgh: 4 of 7 close stools; ASHMORE p.71). Mistress's closet held linen, sweetmeats and glass (ASHMORE p.72).
7. **Back stairs and corridors.** Coleshill had a corridor for separate access to all rooms and back stairs for servants; Roger North praised back stairs as "one of the most important inventions" (Wikipedia, Servants' quarters; Belton: two back stairs). Ham: jib doors and hidden servant stairs (Wikipedia). Old plan has none **[INF]**.
8. **Long gallery.** Top floor on one side, windows on one side and at the ends, doors to other rooms, also circulation (Wikipedia, Long gallery). Sudbury has it on the first floor, which makes Sudbury the modern pattern. Its real use in inventories was often storage or games (ASHMORE p.62-63, 84; see 2.).
9. **Chapel.** Present in earlier and Catholic houses (Scarisbrick, Samlesbury, Bank Hall, Trafford, Wardley); a Protestant 1670 example at Bank Hall (see 2.). Belton: chapel mirrors the kitchen in a two-storey room. Many Restoration houses had none **[INF]**.
10. **Muniment room.** Strong, closed, fire-safe. Braithwait (1588-1673): "a chamber very stronge and close, the walls should be of stone or bricke, the dore should be overplated with iron"; furnished with "drawing boxes, shelves, and standards" with paper inventories. https://castlestudiestrust.org/blog/2020/09/22/archiving-at-a-tudor-castle-thornbury/ . Thornbury (1520s) put it in a tower beside the lord's and lady's bedchambers and the steward's chambers, with six iron-bound chests. For the Restoration house: on the family floor near the steward's room or owner's closet, or a study with presses. Hesketh's study held a gold chain and Dunkenhalgh had "two studies adjoining" a Library Chamber (ASHMORE pp.88, 84). **[GAP]** No inventory of a room called "evidence room" found.
11. **Servants' lodging.** Over service rooms and in garrets (see 1.2); chaff beds for junior and outdoor staff, feather beds for cook, maids and brewer (ASHMORE p.66-67).
12. **Privy.** Close stools and chamber pots in rooms (see 2.). Oakwell great parlour chamber had a garderobe (Wikipedia, Oakwell Hall) **[U]**. **[GAP]** No sourced separate privy or "necessary house" plan for c.1660.

### Fabric notes

- **Floors.** Flagstones in the hall (Eyam). Gypsum plaster floors were "much used in Derbyshire", about 2.5 inches (65 mm) thick (Nicholson, quoted in SPAB), mainly on first and second floors, creamy to grey or pinkish, polished smooth; also in attic servants' quarters; Hardwick upper floors lime-ash (Wikipedia, Lime-ash floor). Matting in the long gallery (Hardwick); rush matting in a bedchamber (Oakwell, 1690, Wikipedia).
- **Walls.** Wainscot oak panelling was fashionable, often treated as furniture, painted in geometric patterns (heritagewoodworkconsultancy.com). Tapestry and Arras, gilt leather (Ham 1675), "bayes" hangings (Dunkenhalgh). Plain plaster or limewash in service rooms **[INF]**.
- **Windows.** Mullioned stone (Eyam; Belton basement and attic). Coleshill originals mullion casements, openings about 5 ft wide, glass panes 5 x 7 in **[U, from RBH, wording ambiguous]**. Sash windows are first documented in England in the 1670s (Ham; Palace House; Wikipedia Sash window), so a 1660 house has casements. Window cushions and curtains (ASHMORE).
- **Heating.** Coal and turf, wood. Coal and turf stores in houses (Middleton 1618; ASHMORE p.86). Grate with andirons: Bank Hall's iron grate cost 5 pounds.

## 3. LIGHT

All from ASHMORE pp.85-86 unless stated.
- **Day.** Windows. Hardwick: "more glass than wall" (Wikipedia). Window cushions and curtains. Looking glasses as light reflectors **[INF]**; at least 12 at Dunkenhalgh 1679, vs 3-4 before 1640 (ASHMORE p.71).
- **Night.**
  - Tallow candles were the commonest. Rushlights ("candle rysshes") were still in use early on; a "shelf for candle Rushes" stood in the deyhouse at Middleton 1618. Wax candles in the best rooms and in the lady's closet (Rufford 1620; Bank Hall Great Parlour 1632: "foure wax candles with floweres").
  - Candles were made in the house: a "tallow trough and matchett" at Dunkenhalgh 1679, and 512 pounds of tallow at Lytham 1634. Candle chests stored them.
  - Candlesticks: Rufford 1620 had 6 silver, 21 pewter, 1 brass; Thomas Tyldesley 1556 18 brass and 3 tin. Placement: one "at the goinge uppe into the chamber over the hall", one "at the kychin doore", a brass three-light candlestick in the hall (Wardley 1554-56). Derby 1651: three wall candlesticks with branches; a hanging candlestick of silver.
  - Snuffers in the dining chamber (Rufford).
- **Fire.** Open hearth in the kitchen; grates in main rooms. Haddon kitchen was "ferociously hot" (britishheritage.com).
- **Ceremonial.** Candlelit saloon (Sudbury NT visitor wording, via search).

## 4. Conflicts and weak points to flag to the builder

1. Coleshill's date: c.1650 (Wikipedia Servants' quarters), c.1650-57 (Pinterest caption), c.1658-62 (Wikipedia Roger Pratt), c.1660 (Wikipedia Coleshill). Treat as c.1650-62.
2. Sudbury gallery: 167 ft vs 138 ft. Sudbury is red brick, not Derbyshire stone; Eyam is gritstone. The brief's stone-country setting follows Eyam for fabric and Sudbury for plan.
3. Haddon gallery attributed to Smythson on the Hall's own site; unverified.
4. The Coleshill ground-floor corner arrangement (bedchamber, withdrawing chamber, parlour with inner closets, steward's room in the fourth corner) came from search summaries, origin not pinned. Verify against Girouard or Gunther.
5. Everything for pantry, scullery, servants' hall, evidence room, study and privy plan is thin. Choose sensible defaults and mark them fictional.
6. Ashmore's houses are larger than a typical mid-gentry Derbyshire hall. Scale down room counts.
