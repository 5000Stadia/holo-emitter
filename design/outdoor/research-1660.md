# Outdoor look of a c.1660 Midlands gentry seat and a pre-Fire London street: research brief

Scope: forecourt, hillside, drive, the London street, and the boundary between them. Looks only, not mechanics. ## A. The seat and its landscape (Derbyshire/Peak-fringe model)

**Region.** "Limestone/gritstone Midlands" = Derbyshire Peak and fringe. Use it as the model, not Northamptonshire ironstone. Ironstone (brown-orange) and Collyweston slates belong further east **[U]**.

### A1. Materials and colours
- Derbyshire houses of the 17th and 18th c. are typically limestone with gritstone dressings and stone slate roofs; Fairfield cottage c.1600 and Flagg Hall (late 17th c.) are cited examples (Wikipedia, Listed buildings in Fairfield and Flagg, via search) **[U]**.
- Hassop Hall (early 17th c., Eyre family): limestone with sandstone dressings and quoins. Its Welsh-slate roof is a 19th-c. change, so do not copy it (Wikipedia, Hassop Hall; DHER MDR4062) **[U]**.
- Eyam Hall (1671-76, Wright family): "an unspoilt example of a gritstone Jacobean manor house" (Wikipedia, Eyam Hall) **[S]**. Gritstone is buff to grey-brown and darkens with soot **[INF]**.
- Beauchief Hall, Sheffield, 1671 (Historic England list text, britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101246567): coursed squared stone and ashlar, ashlar dressings, hipped stone-slate roof **[S]**. Good gritstone-area model of a seat of the exact date.
- **Chosen:** walls warm grey-buff limestone with darker gritstone quoins, mullions and copings. Roofs are stone slate, grey-green with moss, in diminishing courses (large at eaves, small at ridge). Cottages and barns may be thatched or heather-thatched **[INF]**.

### A2. Forecourt, walls, piers, gates
- Stone walls often partly enclosed forecourts, "dignified mansion approaches", and made garden compartments. Gardens of modest houses sat in "one or more walled compartments" (Isle of Wight Gardens Trust, "Country House Gardens and Parks 1550-1660") **[S]**.
- **Beauchief forecourt (the best Derbyshire-area detail found) [S]:** coursed squared stone garden walls enclosing about 70 x 25 m. The higher side walls have rubble coping. The lower front wall has chamfered ashlar coping topped by wrought-iron railing. Gate piers are square ashlar with cornices. Finials are pyramids on four balls, or plain pyramids, or busts. The gates are a pair of wrought-iron gates with an overthrow carrying a coat of arms. The doorway is reached by segmental steps on either side of a landing with a moulded balustrade.
- **Chosen dimensions [INF]:** forecourt wall about 2.4 m (higher side walls), 1.0-1.2 m with iron railing on the entrance front; piers about 2.8-3.2 m high, 0.6 m square; gate leaves about 3.0 m total width, drive gate wide enough for a coach (about 3.5 m).
- Britannia Illustrata shows "coaches pulling into forecourts", gates, toolsheds, parterres and radiating avenues (Wikipedia) **[S]**. The views are 1695-1709, a generation after 1660; for 1660 use the older pattern of walled compartments and simpler layouts **[INF]**.

### A3. Avenues
- Avenues "driven through woods or planted across fields" are documented c.1700 (Wikipedia, Britannia Illustrata) **[S]**; Dunham Massey print: "a long, straight, tree lined avenue" to an obelisk (Chetham's Library blog) **[S]**.
- At 1660 avenues were an upper-gentry novelty (Evelyn's Sylva 1664 pushed planting; I could not read it **[U]**). **Chosen:** a short single-row or double-row avenue (about 100-150 m) of elm and lime to the forecourt gate, young trees about 6 m tall with stakes, plus one old pre-existing tree-lined approach (ash, oak). Wikipedia sources did not list species; elm/lime is **[INF]** from later practice.

### A4. Gardens and outbuildings
- Walled gardens in brick or stone, orchards flanking the formal garden, bowling greens, mounts (popular in Tudor times and still built early C17), fishponds as an "essential garden feature", deer parks and warrens (Isle of Wight Gardens Trust page) **[S]**. A bowling green with a building on a raised mound (possibly a dovecote) is on an Appuldurcombe 1720 engraving, same source **[S]**.
- **Chosen layout [INF]** (dovecote: round or square stone, 4-6 m, lantern roof; a status symbol, sited clear of trees, Wikipedia Dovecote **[S]**): forecourt in front of the entrance; stables and farm courts to one side behind a wall, out of the view from the front windows; kitchen garden and orchard on the sunny flank; bowling green and dovecote at the garden edge; fishponds downslope. (Service courts to the side is **[INF]**; no plans read.)

### A5. Fields, hedges, walls, ridge and furrow
- Peak Fringe: "enclosure of open fields by hedgerows and dry stone walls was underway by the 16th and 17th centuries"; "exceptionally high survival" of 17th-c. isolated farmsteads (Historic England farmstead statement, via search) **[U]**.
- Midlands lowland: first Act in support of enclosure 1603, several more in the 17th c., when live hedges were "planted in large numbers"; hawthorn quickset (Markham 1614); Worlidge recommended white-thorn and holly; elm and other timber trees were often planted in hedges (binghamheritage.org.uk, britainexpress.com, via search) **[U]**. The Parliamentary Acts proper mostly come after 1750 (same source) **[U]**.
- Ridge and furrow: ridges 3-22 yards (3-20 m) apart; furlong about 200 m; strips 4.6-20 m wide; medieval ridges over 1.8 m high, now up to 0.6 m (so c.1660 about 0.3-0.8 m **[INF]**); wheat on the ridge tops, peas and dredge in the furrows; survives where arable became sheep-walk from the 15th c. (Wikipedia, Ridge and furrow) **[S]**.
**Chosen:** field walls about 1.2-1.4 m high, 0.5-0.6 m wide at base, limestone, uneven; irregular small closes near the village and big rectilinear pasture closes higher up. Pre-parliamentary enclosure walls are irregular, lumpy, not the straight grid of the 1760s+ **[INF]**.
- **Chosen hillside mix [INF]:** near the house, hedged or walled pasture closes; further out, unhedged open strips with ridge and furrow, trackways and headlands; sheep and some cattle; a rick or two by the farm court; scattered ash, oak, hawthorn; thorn scrub on steep slopes; a coppice wood (hazel, oak standards).
- Celia Fiennes, 1697: Peak hills steep, "the surface of the earth looks barren" (via search) **[U]**. Supports bare, thin-soiled hilltops.

### A6. Roads and the drive
- Great North Road near Highgate "deeply rutted and spread onto adjoining land" (Wikipedia, Turnpike trusts) **[S]**. Maintenance by parish statute labour under Tudor statutes **[S]**. Heavy carts and carriages "led to serious deterioration" in the 17th c. **[S]**.
- **Chosen:** the lane from the seat to the road is unmetalled, 4-5 m wide between hedge banks, wheel ruts 0.2-0.3 m deep, puddle holes in the gaps, flints and limestone chips thrown into the worst ruts; hollowed 1-2 m below the bank where it climbs (a holloway) **[INF]**. The private drive to the gate is gravelled or hard-packed, 4-5 m **[INF]**. Field gates: oak five-bar, hung on stone gate-stoops or oak posts; stone stiles ("squeeze" stiles in walls) **[INF]**.
- Deer park: Chatsworth's park is on a 1617 map (Parks & Gardens UK) **[S]**. **Chosen:** none; a stone-walled paddock instead **[INF]**.

## B. A London street c.1660

### B1. Widths
- **No pre-Fire width table found** in the sources I could read. The rebuilding Act's numbers are the best proxy: passages under 14 ft (4.3 m) to be widened; Water Lane (a new street) 24 ft (7.3 m) (Statutes of the Realm vol.5 pp.603-612, British History Online) **[S]**. Wren proposed 90 ft (27 m) avenues in his plan (not built) **[U]**. The 1667 Gazette: "Fleet-street, Cheapside, Cornhill, and all other eminent streets" were to be wider "to prevent the mischief one side may receive from the other by fire" **[U]**.
- **Chosen:** by-lanes 2.5-3.5 m; lanes of note 4-5 m; main streets (Fleet St, Cheapside, Holborn) 9-12 m between fronts, with the Strand wider at about 12-15 m. Reason: people on top floors could "shake hands through the windows" (fireoflondon.org.uk) **[S]**, which only works for lanes.

### B2. Paving, kennels, lanterns, dirt
- The 1662 Streets Act: owners must pave "before their houses into the middle of the street"; a channel (kennel) runs down the middle; commissioners may remove "Sheds Stalls Bulks Stoops Posts or Wall beyond the old Foundations"; no hooping barrels or sawing timber in the street; "Sea-coal ashes dust dirt" to be kept in until the scavengers' cart, announced "by a Bell Horne Clapper"; householders must hang "Candles or Lights in Lanthornes" from autumn to early spring from dusk to 9 pm (legislation.gov.uk/aep/Cha2/14/2) **[S]**. The earlier 1416 order for candle lanterns is the same duty (via search) **[U]**.
- Surface (pebbles/cobbles) is not stated in what I read. **Chosen:** rounded river pebbles (about 80-150 mm) set in sand, crowned at the sides with the central kennel lower than the house-side, mud and straw, dung heaps, cart ruts; flat stones only in front of rich houses (post-Fire Act specifies "broad flatt Stone" under balconies, so it was a new standard) **[S/INF]**.
- Smoke: Evelyn, Fumifugium (1661): "such a cloud of sea-coal, as if there be a resemblance of hell upon earth" **[U]**. Use a hazy brown-grey air, soot-darkened stone and plaster.

### B3. Houses
- Brick and stone houses existed, but "many houses were made of wood" and leaned over the street (fireoflondon.org.uk) **[S]**. Cheapside: "buildings three, four, and even five stories tall, whose shopfronts were open to the light" (MoEML Cheapside) **[S]**.
- 229 Strand (early C17; Historic England listing, via search): jettied timber frame, plastered, four storeys, three windows wide; first-floor overhang; second floor "coved out as a further jetty"; ground floor shopfront set back under the overhang **[U]**.
- Staple Inn, Holborn (1585, survived 1666): timber frame, gables, leaded windows **[U]**; the Holborn-end model.
- Posts and stoops existed (the 1662 Act bans new ones past old foundations); downpipes did not, as the 1667 Act first requires "Party pipes" **[S/INF]**.
- Jetties: bressummer on cantilevered joists, curved brackets, dragon beam at the corner; banned in London in 1667 (Wikipedia, Jettying) **[S]**. **Chosen** projection about 0.45-0.6 m per storey, with two stepped jetties on tall houses **[INF]**.
- Heights: the 1667 Act's storey heights are the post-Fire minimum, but old houses were similar: by-lane houses two storeys plus cellar and garret (ground 9 ft = 2.7 m, first 9 ft); principal streets four storeys at 10, 10.5, 9, 8.5 ft (3.0, 3.2, 2.7, 2.6 m) **[S]**. Pre-Fire jetties let the top storey rise to about 12-14 m **[INF]**.
- Plot width: Spitalfields 16 ft (4.9 m); Limehouse 17 ft; a Borough High Street counter 36 ft (Survey of London vols 22, 27, via search) **[U]**. **Chosen:** 4.5-6 m for ordinary shops, 8-10 m for great houses and inns.
- Walls: **no evidence timbers were blackened before the 19th c.**; timbers silver-grey when left, or limewashed; panels pale yellow, ochre, or sand-pink limewash (blackandwhitetrail.org.uk; Herefordshire Through Time, via search) **[U]**. **Chosen:** oak silver-grey to brown, panels in cream, ochre and pink limewash; no black-and-white.
- Roofs: thatch banned in London since 1212, with roofs of "tiles shingles, boards or lead" (buildinghistory.org via search) **[U]**. Use clay plain tile, red-brown, and some lead flats; in 1660 many houses had still timber and plaster **[INF]**.

### B4. Shop fronts, signs, street furniture
- Shopfronts: an opening protected by two horizontal shutters, the upper folding up as an awning or "pent roof" and the lower folding down as a stall board or counter; "an awning over a projecting stall was common" (buildingconservation.com; heritagecalling.com, via search/fetch) **[S]**. Post-Fire Act allowed stall boards to project "eleaven inches and noe more" in high streets, which says earlier stalls projected further (Statutes of the Realm) **[S]**. Glazing in shopfronts is "late 17th century" and not yet standard **[S]**.
- Signs: signs swung from wrought-iron brackets, some carved and gilded (Spitalfields Life; VAM via search) **[U]**. A French visitor c.1698: signs "commonly very large, and jut out so far that in some narrow streets they ... run across almost quite to the other side ... carving and gilding" **[U]**. Laws to remove or fix signs date from the 1760s. **Chosen:** boards 0.6-0.9 m wide, brackets projecting 1-1.5 m, a few gilded.
- Cheapside had the Great and Little Conduits, the Standard and the Eleanor Cross (MoEML) **[S]**.

## C. How the two meet

### C1. The road
- Ogilby's Britannia (1675): first road atlas of England and Wales, 100 plates, strip maps at 1 inch to the mile, standard mile of 1,760 yards. Plate 40 "The Road from London to Darby" runs from Stony Stratford via Northampton, Brixworth, Market Harborough, Oadby, Leicester, Mountsorrel and Kegworth to Derby (Welland Antique Maps; Northamptonshire HER 6745/1, via search) **[U]**. I could not read how the strip maps draw hedges or walls. **Chosen:** in the open Midlands the road runs between hedges or open commons, with milestones not yet standard (they come after 1700) **[INF]**.
- **The brief's premise needs a correction:** turnpikes did exist in 1660. The first, 1663, in Hertfordshire/Cambridgeshire/Huntingdonshire (Wadesmill to Stilton), "for 11 years" (15 Cha. 2 c.1; Wikipedia, Turnpike trusts) **[S]**. None in the Midlands at 1660. A toll gate at a bridge or a town is possible; a turnpike gate on the lane is not.

### C2. Suburbs and bars
- The City's liberties ended at **bars**: "usually consisting of posts and chains", at Holborn and Whitechapel; Strype: "there were only posts, rails, and a chain, such as are now in Holborn, Smithfield, and Whitechapel bars" (via search, Wikipedia, A London Inheritance) **[U/S]**.
- **Temple Bar before Wren's 1672 gate:** "a house of timber erected across the street, with a narrow gateway and an entry on the south side of it under the house"; earlier a posts-and-chain bar; by 1351 a timber archway with a small prison above it (A London Inheritance; Wikipedia, Temple Bar) **[S]**. Wren's Portland-stone gate (1669-72) is *after* 1660. Use the timber house.
- **Holborn Bars:** west of Brooke Street, marking the liberties' edge; tolls were collected from non-freeman carts and carriages at six bars, with Temple Bar and Whitechapel the most profitable (Old and New London vol.2, BHO) **[S]**. Gray's Inn Lane runs north "with fair buildings, and many tenements on both sides leading to the fields towards Highgate and Hampstead"; Holborn Middle Row of small houses blocked the street; Holborn was the road to Tyburn (BHO; Grub Street Project) **[S]**.
- **Chosen boundary for the world join [INF]:** (1) hedged lane leaves the seat's gate and runs 2-3 km of ruts; (2) a toll or gate at a bridge, town edge or hamlet ("last inn"): a coaching inn courtyard, hanging sign, timber gallery; (3) the road thickens into a ribbon of houses; (4) Holborn Bars: two rows of posts joined by rails and a chain across 8-10 m of road, with a small watch-house; (5) beyond it the London street, jettied and sign-hung. Because bars are a chain not a gate, they work as a soft boundary, and the timber Temple Bar house works as a hard one if a gate is wanted.
