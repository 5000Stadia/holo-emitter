# The manor, in the words a player is given

Every line of prose the painted navigation world can put in front of a person, read in one
sitting. There are 114 of them: one arrival and one refusal for each of the manor's 55 ways
through, plus the four that answer a name the world does not hold.

**The question this page asks.** Distinctness is guaranteed by construction — every line names
both rooms — so the four machine clauses that hold this prose (it resolves, it is not a wildcard, it
is pairwise distinct, it carries no duplicate refusal) are satisfied by a mail merge as readily as by
writing. Nothing checks whether it reads as one voice at this length. That is what a person is for,
and it is why the whole set is here rather than a sample.

**What it may not do, and what an artifact critic caught it doing.** The picture draws no windows,
no hearths and no furniture — the plan holds sixty-nine such carriers and the grid paints every one
as plain wall — so prose that named them told a player about things the room does not show. Twelve
clauses were rewritten for that: the great hall no longer has roof timbers over a flat 2.8 m
ceiling, the long gallery no longer runs "window after window", the kitchen's great hearth is no
longer a mouth in the wall. What the lines still evoke is air, sound, use and proportion, which an
empty room does not contradict.

## The four that answer a name the world does not hold

- **toggle.*.refused_unknown** — Nothing of that description offers itself to your hand.
- **take.*.refused_unknown** — You reach, and your hand closes on nothing of the sort.
- **go.*.refused_unknown** — No such passage is to be found; the walls keep their counsel.
- **turn.*.refused** — The room offers no other aspect; you face all there is to face.
